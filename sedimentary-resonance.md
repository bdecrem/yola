# Sedimentary Resonance

*An algorithmic philosophy of time laid down in strata — where geometry sleeps beneath turbulence, and every layer remembers the one beneath it.*

## The Movement

Every abstract painting is a lie told in two dimensions: it claims depth, it claims time, it claims weight, and the eye accepts the lie willingly. **Sedimentary Resonance** refuses to lie smoothly. It insists that a canvas is a core sample — that what the viewer sees on the surface is the topmost layer of an archive, and that beneath every calm band of color there is geological noise, compressed history, and the faint vibration of forces that deposited it. The movement holds that abstraction is not the absence of subject matter; it is the presence of *process*, and process is the one subject that can never be painted literally, only demonstrated.

The philosophy divides the world into two registers: the **crystalline** and the **turbulent**. The crystalline register is geometry at rest — chord structures, rota­­tional symmetry, intervals drawn from harmonic ratios, forms that a mathematician would recognize as *resolved*. The turbulent register is weather — layered value-noise drifting across the field, advecting everything loose, eroding edges, redistributing pigment. A painting in this movement is the negotiation between these two registers: hard-edged resonance rising out of soft-edged chaos, the way crystals grow out of the vapor they condensed from. Neither register may dominate; the meticulous calibration of their tension is the entire art. An algorithm that lets one win produces either a diagram or mud — and mud, here, is a technical term of failure, not a medium.

Time enters laterally. Strata accumulate from the bottom of the canvas upward, each band a sedimentation event: thousands of particles released along a horizon, advected by the noise field, leaving streaks whose length, density, and curvature are the seismograph of the layer's deposition. Early strata are compressed — tight intervals, muted frequencies, the deep-time register. Later strata widen, brighten, occasionally rupture. Every streak is a record; every record is honest about the conditions that made it. The artist's craft lives in the *rate* of change between strata — painstakingly tuned gradients of interval, saturation, and drift that make the archive legible to the eye as rhythm, so the viewer reads the canvas the way a geologist reads a road cut: not as decoration, but as testimony.

Color obeys tonal music. The system is tuned to a narrow pentatonic band — warm earths and oxidized reds against deep green-blues, the complementary tension of sediment against mineral — and all color is selected by quantized bands, never continuously. Discrete color steps give each stratum its own "note," and the eye performs harmony across them, hearing a chord where the algorithm laid down an interval. When a stratum is *dominant* — carrying unusually high energy — it modulates the local palette, bending neighboring strata toward it slightly, the way a loud chord in a room is still humming after it stops. These tonal shadows are what make the archive feel sung rather than merely stacked.

Finally, the movement demands patience of its maker. The emergent archive must look *found*, not fabricated — as if the algorithm had been running for millennia and the viewer arrived exactly one moment too late to see a layer being laid down. Every parameter — stratum count, particle budget, noise octaves, drift rates, rupture probability, the pentatonic band itself — must appear as the product of countless hours of quiet, obsessive refinement: a meticulously crafted instrument played with the restraint of a master. Beauty here is not what the canvas shows. Beauty is the evidence of an equilibrium that the canvas could only have reached through deep, disciplined, computational time.
